There is a habit I keep having to relearn: the temptation to pick a model first and look at the data second. You already know GARCH handles volatility clustering, so you reach for GARCH, and only afterward check whether the clustering in your data has the shape GARCH can actually fit.

This post is the other order. I had a concrete forecasting problem — Kalshi’s 15-minute Bitcoin markets — and before fitting a single model I asked the data six questions, each aimed at a specific fork in the design. Then I fit six candidate models and scored them honestly.

The short version: five of the six questions had answers that ruled something out, the sixth found real structure, and the market still beat every model I built. That last part is the most useful thing in the post.

The market

Kalshi lists a Bitcoin contract that closes every 15 minutes. Each one asks a single question — will the BTC index be at or above some strike when this market closes? — and settles to yes or no. You can buy either side at any point during the market’s life, in cents, where the price is effectively the market’s probability estimate.

So the modeling problem is: given everything observed up to now, what is \(P(\text{settle} \ge K)\)?

The wrinkle that makes this interesting is what “settle” means.

The settlement mechanic

Settlement is not the last price. It is the mean of the 60 whole-second index prints in the final minute before close. That single fact reshapes the problem, so it is worth verifying rather than trusting.

ImportantThe join direction is the whole ballgame

closest(time >= book_time) takes the most recent quote at or before each second. Flipping it to closest(time <= book_time) looks almost identical and is completely different: it grabs the nearest quote at or after each second, which means that whenever a second had no trade, the “market price” you compare against comes from a trade that had not happened yet.

I check it explicitly rather than trusting the code to read correctly:

Q3. How fat are the tails?

This one is decisive rather than suggestive. If I compute \(P(\text{settle} \ge K)\) from a Normal CDF and the returns are not Normal, every probability I produce is wrong in the tails — which is exactly where the mispriced contracts live.

Q5. Is it a random walk?

Model 1 in any forecasting problem should be “the current price is the forecast, and uncertainty grows like \(\sqrt{\text{time}}\).” The variance ratio tests that directly: if returns are a random walk, variance over \(q\) seconds is exactly \(q\) times the 1-second variance, so the ratio is 1.

Q6. What actually happens after a jump?

This is the question the other five were setting up, and it is the only one that found exploitable structure. Take every sharp 10-second move — six standard deviations or more — and track the next two minutes along three series: the raw index, the 60-second trailing mean that actually settles, and the market’s own price.

Read the top panel carefully, because the shape is the entire finding. Offsets are measured from the moment the jump has already happened.

  • The raw index is flat. It jumped, and then it stayed. The move was real, not a spike that reverted.
  • The settlement basis ramps for 60 seconds and then locks. This is not a forecast — it is arithmetic. The 60-second trailing mean still contains pre-jump seconds; as those roll out of the window, the average has no choice but to converge on the new level. By 60 seconds it has fully caught up, and it stays there.

So one minute after a jump, the quantity that determines settlement has moved by a nearly deterministic amount. Now the bottom panel: the market does not follow it.

The market’s path tracks the noisy raw print, not the smooth mechanical ramp of the thing that actually settles. Other participants are reacting to the headline number on the screen rather than to its near-certain consequence for the average.

That gap is the structural reason to think a model could add value here. I want to be precise about what it is and is not: it is evidence of a mechanism, not a demonstrated edge. Whether it is tradeable depends on where the strike sits and what the market price already implies — which is what the scorecard below starts to test.

What the six answers demand

Question Finding Consequence for the model
Q1 Generalises? Jump-and-lag pattern is common Worth modelling, not a one-off
Q2 Trend? Real sustained multi-hour moves A drift state is defensible
Q3 Tails? Excess kurtosis ≈ 49 Student-\(t\) required, Normal ruled out
Q4 Clustering? Two timescales, fast + slow One-parameter GARCH insufficient
Q5 Random walk? Ratio ≈ 1 through 15 min Keep \(\sqrt{t}\) scaling; centre on current price
Q6 After a jump? Settlement basis locks in; market chases raw The structural opening

Which points at a model carrying two latent states — a drift and a stochastic log-volatility — with Student-\(t\) innovations, updated causally. That is a particle filter. So I built one, along with five comparators.

Six models, scored

All six produce the same object: a centre and a \(\sigma\), read through a CDF to get \(P(\text{settle} \ge K)\). They differ only in how they get \(\sigma\) — and the particle filter, which also shifts the centre by its drift state.

Show all six model fits 
suppressPackageStartupMessages(library(rugarch))

mb <- brti |> arrange(time) |> mutate(minute = floor_date(time, "minute")) |>
  summarise(value = first(value), ticker = first(ticker), close_time = first(close_time),
            floor_strike = first(floor_strike), result = first(result),
            yes_mid = first(yes_mid), .by = minute) |>
  arrange(minute) |>
  mutate(ret = coalesce(log(value) - log(lag(value)), 0), ret2 = ret^2,
         remaining_min = as.numeric(difftime(close_time, minute, units = "mins")),
         y = as.integer(result == "yes")) |>
  filter(!is.na(y), remaining_min >= 1, remaining_min <= 15)

ret_min <- mb$ret

# 1. Random walk + rolling realised vol -- the null everything must beat
mb$s_rw <- sqrt(slide_dbl(mb$ret2, mean, .before = 59, .complete = FALSE))

# 2. EWMA / RiskMetrics
mb$s_ewma <- sqrt(accumulate(mb$ret2, \(v, r2) 0.94 * v + 0.06 * r2))

# 3. GARCH(1,1) with Student-t innovations
g_fit <- ugarchfit(
  ugarchspec(variance.model = list(model = "sGARCH", garchOrder = c(1, 1)),
             mean.model = list(armaOrder = c(0, 0), include.mean = FALSE),
             distribution.model = "std"), ret_min, solver = "hybrid")
mb$s_garch <- as.numeric(sigma(g_fit)); g_df <- as.numeric(coef(g_fit)["shape"])

# 4. Component GARCH -- the two-timescale answer to Q4

# 6. Bootstrap particle filter: drift + stochastic log-vol, Student-t
run_pf <- function(r, np, phi_h, mu_h, sigma_h, phi_mu, sigma_mu, nu, frac = 0.5, seed = 1) {
  set.seed(seed); n <- length(r)
  h  <- rnorm(np, mu_h, sigma_h / sqrt(pmax(1 - phi_h^2, 1e-6)))
  mu <- rnorm(np, 0, sigma_mu / sqrt(pmax(1 - phi_mu^2, 1e-6)))
  w  <- rep(1 / np, np); ts <- sqrt(nu / (nu - 2))
  fm <- fv <- numeric(n)
  for (t in seq_len(n)) {
    h  <- mu_h + phi_h * (h - mu_h) + rnorm(np, 0, sigma_h)   # vol state
    mu <- phi_mu * mu + rnorm(np, 0, sigma_mu)                # drift state
    st <- exp(h / 2)
    lk <- dt((r[t] - mu) / (st / ts), df = nu, log = TRUE) - log(st / ts)
    w  <- w * exp(lk - max(lk)); w <- w / sum(w)
    if (1 / sum(w^2) < frac * np) {                            # resample on ESS collapse
      i <- sample.int(np, np, TRUE, prob = w); h <- h[i]; mu <- mu[i]; w <- rep(1 / np, np)
    }
    fm[t] <- sum(w * mu); fv[t] <- sum(w * st)
  }
  tibble(mu = fm, sigma = fv)
}

# ifelse() returns an object the length of its CONDITION, not its branches --

prob_above <- function(log_centre, sigma_h, K, df = Inf) {
  z  <- (log(K) - log_centre) / sigma_h
  df <- rep_len(df, length(z))
  ifelse(is.infinite(df), 1 - pnorm(z), 1 - pt(z, df = df))
}

What I take away

Two results, and the disappointing one is more useful.

The diagnostics paid for themselves. Ruling out the Normal (Q3) and diagnosing two volatility timescales (Q4) were both decisive and neither would have been visible from model output alone — a Normal-CDF model does not announce that its tails are wrong, it just quietly misprices them. Q5 also stopped me writing a mean-reversion story into six data points, which is the kind of thing that reads as insight right up until it costs money.

The market is well priced at these horizons, and sophistication did not change that. From 1 to 15 minutes before close, none of the six models beats the market price, and the most complex model does not even beat the naive baseline. The honest conclusion is that this horizon is information-limited, not model-limited. Adding parameters was not the missing ingredient.

Which sends me back to Q6, the one finding the scorecard never actually tested. The settlement basis locking in after a jump is a mechanical, 60-second phenomenon — and this scorecard scores from 1 to 15 minutes, so it excludes that window entirely by construction. The interesting question is not whether a better volatility model wins at 10 minutes out. It is whether knowing that part of the settlement average has already been observed and cannot change is worth anything in the final seconds, when the market is still looking at the raw print.

That is a different model, a different scoring grid, and its own post.

There is a habit I keep having to relearn: the temptation to pick a model first and
look at the data second. You already know GARCH handles volatility clustering, so you
reach for GARCH, and only afterward check whether the clustering in *your* data has the
shape GARCH can actually fit.

This post is the other order. I had a concrete forecasting problem — Kalshi's 15-minute
Bitcoin markets — and before fitting a single model I asked the data six questions, each
aimed at a specific fork in the design. Then I fit six candidate models and scored them
honestly.

The short version: five of the six questions had answers that ruled something out, the
sixth found real structure, and the market still beat every model I built. That last part
is the most useful thing in the post.

## The market

Kalshi lists a Bitcoin contract that closes every 15 minutes. Each one asks a single
question — *will the BTC index be at or above some strike when this market closes?* — and
settles to yes or no. You can buy either side at any point during the market's life, in
cents, where the price is effectively the market's probability estimate.

So the modeling problem is: given everything observed up to now, what is
$P(\text{settle} \ge K)$?

The wrinkle that makes this interesting is what "settle" means.

## The settlement mechanic

Settlement is **not** the last price. It is the mean of the 60 whole-second index prints
in the final minute before close. That single fact reshapes the problem, so it is worth
verifying rather than trusting.

## The join direction is the whole ballgame

`closest(time >= book_time)` takes the most recent quote at or *before* each second.
Flipping it to `closest(time <= book_time)` looks almost identical and is completely
different: it grabs the nearest quote at or *after* each second, which means that
whenever a second had no trade, the "market price" you compare against comes from a trade
that had not happened yet.

I check it explicitly rather than trusting the code to read correctly:


seconds happen roughly a million times more often than Normal says they should. In plain
terms: if you priced the tails off a Normal you would be selling catastrophically
underpriced insurance all day.

**Decision: Student-$t$, not Normal.** Not a refinement — a requirement.

## Q4. What shape is the volatility clustering?

Everyone knows volatility clusters. The useful question is *how*, because that determines
whether a one-parameter persistence model can fit it.


Returns themselves are essentially uncorrelated — no simple momentum to harvest. Squared
returns are a different story, and the *shape* is the finding: a fast decay over the first
few seconds, then a long, flat, still-significant tail running out to five minutes.

Two timescales. A GARCH(1,1) has exactly one persistence parameter, so it must either fit
the fast decay or the slow tail — it cannot do both. **Decision: allow a two-component
volatility structure** (component GARCH, or a latent stochastic-volatility state).

## Q5. Is it a random walk?

Model 1 in any forecasting problem should be "the current price is the forecast, and
uncertainty grows like $\sqrt{\text{time}}$." The variance ratio tests that directly: if
returns are a random walk, variance over $q$ seconds is exactly $q$ times the 1-second
variance, so the ratio is 1.

```{r}
#| label: q5
#| code-summary: "Show code"

t_num <- as.numeric(cont$time); log_val <- log(cont$value)
q_grid <- c(1, 5, 15, 30, 60, 120, 300, 600, 900, 1800, 3600, 7200, 10800, 21600)

var_at_q <- function(q) {
  bin  <- floor(t_num / q)
  last <- tapply(log_val, bin, \(x) x[length(x)])
  d <- diff(last)
  c(var = var(d, na.rm = TRUE), n = sum(!is.na(d)))
}
vr <- map(q_grid, var_at_q) |> bind_rows() |> mutate(q = q_grid) |>
  mutate(var_ratio = var / (q * var[q == 1]),
         label = c("1s","5s","15s","30s","1m","2m","5m","10m","15m","30m","1h","2h","3h","6h"))

ggplot(vr, aes(q, var_ratio)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 1, linetype = "dashed", colour = "grey40") +
  geom_line(colour = pal[["settle"]]) +
  geom_point(aes(size = n), colour = pal[["settle"]]) +
  scale_x_log10(breaks = q_grid, labels = vr$label) +
  scale_size_continuous(range = c(1, 4), name = "non-overlapping obs", trans = "log10") +
  labs(title = "Variance ratio from 1 second to 6 hours",
       subtitle = "1.0 = pure random walk · point size = how much data backs each estimate",
       x = "aggregation interval (log scale)", y = "Var(q) / (q × Var(1s))") +
  theme(axis.text.x = element_text(angle = 45, hjust = 1))
```

```{r}
#| label: q5-table
#| code-fold: false

  mutate(var_ratio = round(var_ratio, 3)) |> print(n = 14)
```

From 1 second out to about 15 minutes — the entire horizon this contract lives on — the
ratio sits within a few percent of 1. The square-root-of-time assumption is a good
approximation exactly where I need it.

The longer horizons wander away from 1, and it is tempting to read a story into that. I
am not going to, and the point sizes are why: at 6 hours a two-week window gives only
`r vr$n[vr$q == 21600]` non-overlapping observations. That is not enough to distinguish
mean reversion from noise. **Decision: keep $\sqrt{t}$ scaling; treat the long-horizon
wiggle as unmeasured, not as a finding.**

## Q6. What actually happens after a jump?

This is the question the other five were setting up, and it is the only one that found
exploitable structure. Take every sharp 10-second move — six standard deviations or more —
and track the next two minutes along three series: the raw index, the 60-second trailing
mean that actually settles, and the market's own price.

```{r}
#| label: q6
#| fig-height: 6
#| code-summary: "Show code"

df_evt <- brti |> arrange(ticker, time) |> distinct(ticker, time, .keep_all = TRUE) |>
  select(ticker, time, value, kalshi_value, yes_mid) |>
  mutate(row_id = row_number())

W <- 10
df_evt <- df_evt |> group_by(ticker) |>
  mutate(ret_w = log(value) - log(lag(value, W)), last_row = max(row_id)) |> ungroup()

events <- df_evt |>
  filter(!is.na(ret_w), abs(ret_w) > 6 * sd(df_evt$ret_w, na.rm = TRUE),
         !is.na(yes_mid), (last_row - row_id) >= 120, row_id > W)

evt <- map_dfr(0:120, \(o) {
  i <- events$row_id + o
  tibble(
    offset = o,
    raw_bps    = mean((log(df_evt$value[i]) - log(events$value)) * sign(events$ret_w), na.rm = TRUE) * 1e4,
    settle_bps = mean((log(df_evt$kalshi_value[i]) - log(events$kalshi_value)) * sign(events$ret_w), na.rm = TRUE) * 1e4,
    market_c   = mean((df_evt$yes_mid[i] - events$yes_mid) * sign(events$ret_w), na.rm = TRUE)
  )
})

pa <- evt |> select(offset, raw_bps, settle_bps) |>
  pivot_longer(-offset) |>
  mutate(name = recode(name, raw_bps = "raw index",
                       settle_bps = "60s mean (what settles)")) |>
  ggplot(aes(offset, value, colour = name)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c("raw index" = pal[["raw"]],
                                 "60s mean (what settles)" = pal[["settle"]])) +
  labs(title = "After a 6σ jump: the index holds, the settlement basis catches up",
       x = "seconds since jump", y = "move in basis points", colour = NULL)

pb <- ggplot(evt, aes(offset, market_c)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, colour = "grey60", linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_line(colour = pal[["market"]], linewidth = 1) +
  labs(title = "The market's price over the same 120 seconds",
       x = "seconds since jump", y = "move in cents")

pa / pb
```

Read the top panel carefully, because the shape is the entire finding. Offsets are
measured from the moment the jump has *already happened*.

- **The raw index is flat.** It jumped, and then it stayed. The move was real, not a spike
  that reverted.
- **The settlement basis ramps for 60 seconds and then locks.** This is not a forecast —
  it is arithmetic. The 60-second trailing mean still contains pre-jump seconds; as those
  roll out of the window, the average has no choice but to converge on the new level. By
  60 seconds it has fully caught up, and it stays there.

So one minute after a jump, the quantity that determines settlement has moved by a nearly
deterministic amount. Now the bottom panel: the market does not follow it.

```{r}

#| code-fold: false

  settle_move_at_60s_bps = round(evt$settle_bps[evt$offset == 60], 1),
  corr_market_with_raw    = round(cor(evt$market_c, evt$raw_bps), 2),
  corr_market_with_settle = round(cor(evt$market_c, evt$settle_bps), 2))
```

The market's path tracks the noisy raw print, not the smooth mechanical ramp of the thing
that actually settles. Other participants are reacting to the headline number on the
screen rather than to its near-certain consequence for the average.

**That gap is the structural reason to think a model could add value here.** I want to be
precise about what it is and is not: it is evidence of a mechanism, not a demonstrated
edge. Whether it is tradeable depends on where the strike sits and what the market price
already implies — which is what the scorecard below starts to test.

## What the six answers demand

| Question | Finding | Consequence for the model |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 Generalises? | Jump-and-lag pattern is common | Worth modelling, not a one-off |
| Q2 Trend? | Real sustained multi-hour moves | A drift state is defensible |
| Q3 Tails? | Excess kurtosis ≈ `r round(excess_kurt, 0)` | Student-$t$ required, Normal ruled out |
| Q4 Clustering? | Two timescales, fast + slow | One-parameter GARCH insufficient |
| Q5 Random walk? | Ratio ≈ 1 through 15 min | Keep $\sqrt{t}$ scaling; centre on current price |
| Q6 After a jump? | Settlement basis locks in; market chases raw | The structural opening |

Which points at a model carrying two latent states — a drift and a stochastic
log-volatility — with Student-$t$ innovations, updated causally. That is a particle
filter. So I built one, along with five comparators.

## Six models, scored

All six produce the same object: a centre and a $\sigma$, read through a CDF to get
$P(\text{settle} \ge K)$. They differ only in how they get $\sigma$ — and the particle
filter, which also shifts the centre by its drift state.

```{r}
#| label: models
#| code-summary: "Show all six model fits"
suppressPackageStartupMessages(library(rugarch))

mb <- brti |> arrange(time) |> mutate(minute = floor_date(time, "minute")) |>
  summarise(value = first(value), ticker = first(ticker), close_time = first(close_time),
            floor_strike = first(floor_strike), result = first(result),
            yes_mid = first(yes_mid), .by = minute) |>
  arrange(minute) |>
  mutate(ret = coalesce(log(value) - log(lag(value)), 0), ret2 = ret^2,
         remaining_min = as.numeric(difftime(close_time, minute, units = "mins")),
         y = as.integer(result == "yes")) |>
  filter(!is.na(y), remaining_min >= 1, remaining_min <= 15)

ret_min <- mb$ret

# 1. Random walk + rolling realised vol -- the null everything must beat
mb$s_rw <- sqrt(slide_dbl(mb$ret2, mean, .before = 59, .complete = FALSE))

# 2. EWMA / RiskMetrics
mb$s_ewma <- sqrt(accumulate(mb$ret2, \(v, r2) 0.94 * v + 0.06 * r2))

# 3. GARCH(1,1) with Student-t innovations
g_fit <- ugarchfit(
  ugarchspec(variance.model = list(model = "sGARCH", garchOrder = c(1, 1)),
             mean.model = list(armaOrder = c(0, 0), include.mean = FALSE),
             distribution.model = "std"), ret_min, solver = "hybrid")
mb$s_garch <- as.numeric(sigma(g_fit)); g_df <- as.numeric(coef(g_fit)["shape"])

# 4. Component GARCH -- the two-timescale answer to Q4
c_fit <- ugarchfit(
  ugarchspec(variance.model = list(model = "csGARCH", garchOrder = c(1, 1)),
             mean.model = list(armaOrder = c(0, 0), include.mean = FALSE),
             distribution.model = "std"), ret_min, solver = "hybrid")
mb$s_csgarch <- as.numeric(sigma(c_fit)); c_df <- as.numeric(coef(c_fit)["shape"])

# 5. HAR-RV -- realised variance at three horizons, a hard baseline to beat
har <- mb |> mutate(block = floor_date(minute, "15 minutes")) |>
  summarise(rv = sum(ret2), .by = block) |> arrange(block) |>
  mutate(rv_1h = slide_dbl(rv, mean, .before = 3, .complete = TRUE),
         rv_3h = slide_dbl(rv, mean, .before = 11, .complete = TRUE),
         rv_next = lead(rv)) |>
  filter(!is.na(rv_next), !is.na(rv_3h))
h_fit <- lm(rv_next ~ rv + rv_1h + rv_3h, data = har)
mb <- mb |> mutate(block = floor_date(minute, "15 minutes")) |>
  left_join(har |> mutate(p = pmax(predict(h_fit), 1e-12)) |> select(block, p), by = "block") |>
  mutate(s_har = sqrt(coalesce(p, mean(p, na.rm = TRUE)) / 15))

# 6. Bootstrap particle filter: drift + stochastic log-vol, Student-t
run_pf <- function(r, np, phi_h, mu_h, sigma_h, phi_mu, sigma_mu, nu, frac = 0.5, seed = 1) {
  set.seed(seed); n <- length(r)
  h  <- rnorm(np, mu_h, sigma_h / sqrt(pmax(1 - phi_h^2, 1e-6)))
  mu <- rnorm(np, 0, sigma_mu / sqrt(pmax(1 - phi_mu^2, 1e-6)))
  w  <- rep(1 / np, np); ts <- sqrt(nu / (nu - 2))
  fm <- fv <- numeric(n)
  for (t in seq_len(n)) {
    h  <- mu_h + phi_h * (h - mu_h) + rnorm(np, 0, sigma_h)   # vol state
    mu <- phi_mu * mu + rnorm(np, 0, sigma_mu)                # drift state
    st <- exp(h / 2)
    lk <- dt((r[t] - mu) / (st / ts), df = nu, log = TRUE) - log(st / ts)
    w  <- w * exp(lk - max(lk)); w <- w / sum(w)
    if (1 / sum(w^2) < frac * np) {                            # resample on ESS collapse
      i <- sample.int(np, np, TRUE, prob = w); h <- h[i]; mu <- mu[i]; w <- rep(1 / np, np)
    }
    fm[t] <- sum(w * mu); fv[t] <- sum(w * st)
  }
  tibble(mu = fm, sigma = fv)
}
ewma_v <- accumulate(ret_min^2, \(v, r2) 0.94 * v + 0.06 * r2)
ar1 <- arima(log(pmax(ewma_v, 1e-30)), order = c(1, 0, 0))
z <- (ret_min - mean(ret_min)) / sd(ret_min); nu <- max(4.1, 6 / max(mean(z^4) - 3, 0.1) + 4)
top <- vr |> slice_max(var_ratio, n = 1, with_ties = FALSE)
hl <- max(top$q / 60, 1); phi_mu <- exp(-1 / hl)
sigma_mu <- sqrt(max(top$var_ratio - 1, 1e-3) * top$var / hl^2 * (1 - phi_mu^2))
pf <- run_pf(ret_min, 500, unname(ar1$coef["ar1"]), unname(ar1$coef["intercept"]),
             sqrt(ar1$sigma2), phi_mu, sigma_mu, nu)
mb$s_pf <- pf$sigma; mb$mu_pf <- pf$mu
```

```{r}
#| label: probs
#| code-summary: "Show the probability step"

# ifelse() returns an object the length of its CONDITION, not its branches --
# with a scalar df this silently collapses the whole vector. Recycle first.
prob_above <- function(log_centre, sigma_h, K, df = Inf) {
  z  <- (log(K) - log_centre) / sigma_h
  df <- rep_len(df, length(z))
  ifelse(is.infinite(df), 1 - pnorm(z), 1 - pt(z, df = df))
}

h  <- sqrt(mb$remaining_min); lc <- log(mb$value); K <- mb$floor_strike
sc <- mb |> mutate(
  p_market  = yes_mid / 100,
  p_rw      = prob_above(lc, s_rw      * h, K),
  p_ewma    = prob_above(lc, s_ewma    * h, K),
  p_garch   = prob_above(lc, s_garch   * h, K, df = g_df),
  p_csgarch = prob_above(lc, s_csgarch * h, K, df = c_df),
  p_har     = prob_above(lc, s_har     * h, K),
  p_pf      = prob_above(lc + mu_pf * remaining_min, s_pf * h, K, df = nu)
) |> filter(!is.na(p_market))
```

Scoring is by log loss — lower is better — against two reference points. The **base rate**
just predicts the overall yes-frequency and is the floor of usefulness. The **market's own
price** is the bar that matters.

```{r}
log_loss <- function(p, y, eps = 1e-6) {

}

            EWMA = "p_ewma", `RW+RV` = "p_rw", `Particle filter` = "p_pf", HAR = "p_har")

tibble(model = c("Base rate", names(models)),

  arrange(log_loss)
```

Every model lands between the base rate and the market, which is the sane result. But
these are averages over overlapping minutes within the same markets, so I am not going to
call any two of them different without checking. Resampling **whole markets** with
replacement respects that clustering:

```{r}
#| label: bootstrap

eps <- 1e-6
L <- map_dfc(models, \(cc) { p <- pmin(pmax(sc[[cc]], eps), 1 - eps)
                            -(sc$y * log(p) + (1 - sc$y) * log(1 - p)) })
idx <- split(seq_len(nrow(sc)), sc$ticker); tk <- names(idx)

boot <- map_dfr(seq_len(B), \(b) {
```

## What I take away

Two results, and the disappointing one is more useful.

**The diagnostics paid for themselves.** Ruling out the Normal (Q3) and diagnosing two
volatility timescales (Q4) were both decisive and neither would have been visible from
model output alone — a Normal-CDF model does not announce that its tails are wrong, it
just quietly misprices them. Q5 also stopped me writing a mean-reversion story into six
data points, which is the kind of thing that reads as insight right up until it costs
money.

**The market is well priced at these horizons, and sophistication did not change that.**
From 1 to 15 minutes before close, none of the six models beats the market price, and the
most complex model does not even beat the naive baseline. The honest conclusion is that
this horizon is information-limited, not model-limited. Adding parameters was not the
missing ingredient.

Which sends me back to Q6, the one finding the scorecard never actually tested. The
settlement basis locking in after a jump is a mechanical, 60-second phenomenon — and this
scorecard scores from 1 to 15 minutes, so it excludes that window entirely by
construction. The interesting question is not whether a better volatility model wins at 10
minutes out. It is whether knowing that *part of the settlement average has already been
observed and cannot change* is worth anything in the final seconds, when the market is
still looking at the raw print.

That is a different model, a different scoring grid, and its own post.
